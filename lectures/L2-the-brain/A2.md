# A2 · How a brain would read your work

**Agentic Systems Engineering · Lecture 2 · Vizuara · Dr. Rajat Dandekar · Due before Lecture 3 (Sat 7 Nov 2026)**

In Lecture 2 we followed one question through a language model. Emma asked how many city e-bike battery packs
Northbridge has in Manchester. Ask Northbridge, with no tools, answered at once with a number (42, 48, 54 or 142,
depending on the run). Ledgerline says 5,400. Nothing went wrong inside the model. The question was cut into tokens,
turned into vectors, mixed by attention through 24 blocks, and answered one likely token at a time. The input never
held the number, so the model produced a plausible one instead.

This assignment asks you to do the same for **your own work or domain**: the documents people there write, and the
questions they ask. There are no answers to look up. What we grade is careful observation and clear reasoning in
the terms of the lecture.

Use documents you know and are allowed to share. Leave out anything confidential: change names, and never paste
real customer, patient or employee data. If you have no work documents to use, pick a domain you know well from
outside work, such as a club's rules, a school's admission policy or a landlord's tenancy terms.

## Part 1 · How a model would cut your documents (`TOKENS.md`)

Take one or two pages of real text from your domain: a policy, a procedure, a report, a few e-mails.

1. **Cut it.** Paste it into the last cell of NB2 ("Your own documents"). Report the number of characters, words
   and tokens, and the characters per token. Compare it with Northbridge's user manual in NB2 section 2.
2. **What gets split.** List the ten words or codes in your text that split into the most tokens. What kinds of
   words are they: names, codes, numbers, words from another language, jargon? For each kind, say what this means for
   a model that reads them a piece at a time. Think of the way 5,400 is read digit by digit.
3. **Words versus tokens.** Your domain will have words a fixed word vocabulary would never contain, such as product
   codes, new customer names or typos. Give three real examples. Explain in your own words why a token vocabulary
   still handles them, and what it costs.
4. **What it costs.** Estimate how many tokens one realistic question in your domain would send, counting the
   instructions, the question and any documents it needs. Use the lecture's prices (US dollars per million tokens) to say what 1,000 such questions
   a day would cost for a year. Show your working, and remember the thinking tokens from NB2 exercise 3.

## Part 2 · Where it would invent (`INVENT.md`)

1. **Three questions.** Write three questions people in your domain really ask. At least one should need a fact
   that changes daily, like a balance, a status, a stock level or a queue. At least one should need a rule that is
   written down somewhere in your organisation.
2. **Ask a brain alone.** Ask one of them to a chat model with no tools and no documents. You can use your own key
   in NB2, or any chat app with web search and file upload switched off. Give it a short system prompt like Ask
   Northbridge's, saying who it is and today's date. Paste the exact prompt and the exact reply. Then ask the same
   question three more times, in new chats, and paste those replies too.
3. **Trace it.** For one invented detail in those replies, explain where it came from, step by step in the terms of
   the lecture: what was in the input, what the next-token game rewards, what training put into the weights, and
   what the cut-off means for your question. Write 200 to 300 words. Use at least five of these terms correctly:
   token, vector, attention, next-token probability, temperature, sampling, training data, cut-off, system prompt,
   context.
4. **The dangerous one.** Which of your three questions would do the most harm if answered with a confident made-up
   answer? Say who would act on it, what they would do, and how long it would take before anyone noticed.

## Part 3 · What facts it would need (`FACTS.md`)

1. **The missing facts.** For each of your three questions, list the facts that would have to be in the input for
   the answer to be right. Say where each fact lives today: a system, a document, a spreadsheet, or one person's
   memory.
2. **Knowledge or tools.** Sort the facts into two groups. Some can be read from documents that change slowly
   (Lecture 3, knowledge). Others must be looked up live in a system at the moment of the question (Lecture 4,
   tools). Give the reason for each choice.
3. **Test it.** Take one question and put the fact into the prompt yourself, as NB2 exercise 6 does with the stock
   line. Ask again and paste the reply. Did it use the fact? Did it still add anything you did not give it?
