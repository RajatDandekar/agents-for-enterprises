Part one

Why the model does not know Rasoi

Frozen weights, and one way in.

Private, and newer

A model learns in training: months of reading public text, once. Then its weights are frozen.

TrainingAdjusting the model’s weights so it predicts the next token of a huge pile of text. It happens before you ever use the model.

Rasoi’s documents are private. Two of them are dated after September 2026. One changes again on 1 December.

So no amount of “asking nicely” helps. The model has nothing to remember.

Fig. 2. Effective dates from each document’s front-matter in bahi/docs/.

Two places to know from

Its weights, learned in training. Fixed. Rasoi was never in them.

Its context window: the tokens we send on this one call. The model reads them fresh, every time, and forgets them after.

Context windowEverything the model reads on one call: system prompt, conversation, documents, question. Its only working memory.
Fig. 3. If Rasoi’s clause is not among this call’s tokens, it does not exist for the model.

Why not train it on Rasoi?

Fine-tuningTraining a finished model a little more, on your own examples, so the weights change.

It is good at teaching a style or a format. It is a poor way to store rules:

  • 1Stale. Every amendment means training again. Rasoi has had two this year.
  • 2No citation. A weight cannot tell you which clause it came from.
  • 3One model, every reader. HR’s salary bands would be in it for everyone.
What we did not measureThe cost and time of a fine-tuning run. We did not run one: the three problems above are true at any price. Everything else in this lecture is measured.
The idea that remainsLeave the weights alone. Put the right clause into the context window, at the moment of the question.

Same model, same question. The only difference is what is in its context window.

With §6.2 pasted in (363 tokens in all), it names the food-grade rule and cites it. Press the buttons.

We pasted the whole company’s documents too: purchasing plus all eight module kits.

Still right on 40 of 40. But twice the tokens and twice the rupees, for the same answers.

Part three

Retrieval

Find the few paragraphs that answer, and send only those.

RetrievalPicking, from all the documents, the few passages most likely to answer this question.

Once: cut the documents and index them two ways.

Every question: search both indexes, fuse, rerank, keep the top 4.

We now build each box, left to right.

Fig. 10. The pipeline behind search_policy (bahi/agent/retrieval.py).

Cutting the documents

ChunkA piece of a document small enough to be one idea, with enough context to stand on its own.

The naive cut: windows of N words. Move the slider and watch where §6.2 (green underline) lands.

The better cut for a policy: one clause per chunk, whole, with its heading. Its id is its citation.

One chunk, zoomed in

Besides its words, each chunk carries five things:

  • 1an id that is the citation;
  • 2a heading path, searched with the text;
  • 3a version and effective date, for freshness;
  • 4an audience, for who may read it;
  • 5a status: in force, replaced, or not yet.
Fig. 11. The chunker cut the corpus into 251 chunks; Kavita’s index holds 247 (logs/retrieval_eval.json).

Clauses or windows?

33 of our test questions have a known right clause. Does a search find it, whole, in its top 5?

By clause, more often, for every search method. Windows lose most with embeddings, which blur a window that mixes three rules.

And a window has no clause id, so the answer could not cite it.

Fig. 12. logs/retrieval_eval.json → fixed_vs_section, summary.

Keyword search

The oldest idea: a chunk that shares the question’s words is probably about it.

First, turn the question into terms: lower case, drop the small words, cut plurals.

We teach it on the question as Suresh would ask it (scenario 1). We come back to Kavita’s own words in Part four.

Step 1: counting, with a ceiling

A word that appears twice is more telling than once, but not twice as telling.

BM25 bends the count: tf·(k₁+1) / (tf + k₁·L). It rises fast, then flattens at k₁+1. Slide k₁.

L is the chunk’s length against the average. A long chunk mentions everything a little, so its words count less. Slide it.

Fig. 13. k₁ = 1.2, b = 0.75 in retrieval.BM25. The length slider starts at §6.2’s own: 47 terms against an average of 33.9.

Step 2: rare words count more

“supplier” is in many chunks, so finding it says little. “litre” is in few.

idf(t) = ln(1 + (N − nₜ + ½) / (nₜ + ½)) N = 247 chunks Kavita may read nₜ = chunks that contain t

These are the real counts in Kavita’s index.

Step 3: add it up

BM25(q, d) = Σₜ idf(t) · tf·(k₁+1) / (tf + k₁·(1 − b + b·|d|/avgdl))

For §6.2, term by term, with the real numbers.

Six words earn points. The sum is §6.2’s score for this question.

BM25A keyword score: rare shared words count most, repeats saturate, long chunks are discounted.

BM25 ranks every chunk

The same sum for all 247 chunks, highest first.

§6.2 comes fourth.

First is Supplier Policy §4.2, helped by “better”. Kavita meant a better price. §4.2 says “grade 304 or better”. Keywords cannot tell meanings apart.

Fig. 14. Real BM25 scores, logs/retrieval_eval.json → s1.bm25.

Embeddings, again

In Lecture 2 an embedding turned a token into a list of numbers. An embedding model does it for a whole passage: ours, bge-small-en-v1.5, gives 384 numbers.

Passages that mean similar things get similar lists. Each dot is one real chunk, squeezed from 384 numbers to 2. The star is a question.

It runs on a CPU, needs no key, and gives the same numbers every run.

Cosine similarity

Cosine similarityHow closely two lists of numbers point the same way: their dot product divided by both lengths. 1 = same direction, 0 = unrelated.

Turn the angle. Length does not matter; only direction does.

That is why it suits meaning: a long clause and a short question can still point the same way.

384 numbers, added up

The same formula, on the real vectors of the question and §6.2.

Multiply them dimension by dimension; add the products. Blue pushes towards “same”, ember away.

Over all 384, §6.2 gets 0.7595: the highest of all 247 chunks for this question.

Fig. 15. Vectors from logs/retrieval_eval.json → s1.qvec, s1.c62vec.

Where each method goes blind

Rank of the right clause among Kavita’s 247 chunks.

Keywords miss meaning“better price” matched “grade 304 or better”. BM25 put §6.2 fourth.
Embeddings miss codesERR-PO-0412 is letters and digits, not a meaning. Embeddings did not find §17.2 in their top 10. BM25 found it second.

Use both: fuse the ranks

BM25 scores are around 20; cosines around 0.7. You cannot add them.

Reciprocal rank fusionEach list gives an item 1 / (k + its rank). Add them up. Ranks, not scores, so units do not matter.

§6.2: rank 4 in BM25, rank 1 in vectors → 1/64 + 1/61 = 0.03202. Try a different k.

Fig. 16. Top 20 from each list, logs/retrieval_eval.json → s1.hybrid.

Rerank the top 20

RerankerA model that reads the question and one passage together and scores how well the passage answers it.

An embedding reads each text alone. A reranker sees both at once, so it can tell “better price” from “grade 304 or better”.

Too slow for 247 chunks; fine for the fused top 20. Ours: ms-marco MiniLM, on a CPU.

Fig. 17. Its score is a logit: 0 is even odds, below 0 probably not relevant.

Four rankings

Same question, real scores. Each step moves §6.2.

BM25: 4th. Embeddings: 1st.

Fused: 2nd, just behind §4.1.

Reranked: 1st, and the only clause above 0.

Ranking, measured

All 33 questions with a known right clause. No chat model involved, so it is free to measure: do it every time you change the chunker.

recall@kThe share of questions whose right clause is in the top k results.
Hybrid + rerank put the right clause first for 28 of 33, and in the top 5 for all 33. Neither method alone got all 33 into the top 5.
Fig. 18. logs/retrieval_eval.json → summary.

Check yourself

  1. 1Why does BM25 give “litre” more weight than “supplier”?
    Fewer chunks contain it, so its idf is higher: finding it says more.
  2. 2A question asks about “ERR-SCR-0221”. Which search is more likely to find the right clause, and why?
    BM25: the code is an exact string. Embeddings carry meaning, and a code has little.
  3. 3Why fuse ranks rather than add the scores?
    The scores are in different units (BM25 about 20, cosine about 0.7). Ranks are comparable.
  4. 4Why not rerank all 247 chunks?
    A reranker reads each pair together, one model run per chunk. The fused top 20 is cheap enough.

A citation is a key

CitationThe id of the clause an answer relies on, written next to the claim: [POL-PUR-001 §6.2].

Because each chunk’s id is its citation, a citation is not decoration. It is something we can look up.

Kavita can click it and read the clause. Rohit can check the answer before he approves. Audit can trace it a year later.

What the answer said“… gaskets are food-contact parts and may only be bought from a supplier holding a valid food-grade certificate on file in Bahi POL-PUR-001 §6.2 POL-SUP-003 §4.1 …”
Two questions to ask of every citationDoes the clause exist? Was it in front of the model when it wrote the answer?

Checking them, in code

CITE = r"((?:POL|MAN|AMD|HR)-[A-Z0-9-]+)\s*§\s*(\d+(?:\.\d+)?)" def check_citations(answer, context_ids, corpus_ids): cites = extract_citations(answer) return {"invented": [c for c in cites if c not in corpus_ids], "ungrounded": [c for c in cites if c in corpus_ids and c not in context_ids]}

Invented: no such clause. Ungrounded: a real clause the model never received: a lucky guess.

Fig. 20. The second answer was edited by us, by hand, to show the check firing.

Check yourself

  1. 1The retriever is right on 40 of 40 test questions. Why did it miss Kavita’s?
    Her words never name the rule (food, certificate). Retrieval finds text that looks like the question. The test questions used the rule’s words.
  2. 2An answer cites [POL-PUR-001 §6.2], a real clause, but §6.2 was not in its prompt. Is that fine?
    No. It is ungrounded: the model guessed a real id. Next time it may guess wrong.
  3. 3Why search one short query per rule rather than one long one?
    A reranker scores the pair. A query mixing four topics matches no single clause well; our long query put §6.2 under the cut.
Part five

The LLM wiki

Compile the documents once, into pages a model can browse.

LLM wikiPages about each topic, written once by a model from the documents, every rule cited, with an index. Andrej Karpathy’s idea.

The model does the reading before anyone asks. A code check lints it: every citation must exist, every link must resolve.

At question time it reads the index, picks up to three pages, and answers.

Fig. 22. retrieval.Wiki: compile(), update(), answer().

Eighteen pages, linked

The wiki our compile wrote. A circle is a page; a line is a link the model wrote. Click a page.

Same question, Kavita’s own words, no rewriting.

The model reads the index, sees a page called “Food-grade compliance”, and knows that gaskets for cookers belong there. A search engine cannot make that jump; a model reading a table of contents can.

Part six

Who may read what, since when

Access control and freshness.

Filter first

ClearanceThe set of audiences a person’s role may read. Kavita, PUR-EXEC: all staff and purchase. Not HR.

Build each person’s index from only what they may read. What is not in her index cannot reach her context window, whatever the prompt says.

Fig. 25. Retriever(permissions=True) filters by audience before ranking.

Rules change

An amendment names the clause it replaces and the date it starts. The retriever reads that date against Bahi’s clock.

In force: the old clause leaves the index. Not yet in force: both stay, each labelled with its dates. Drag the date.

A3: the memory for your module

Due before Lecture 4, Sun 8 Nov. Feeds the 15 retrieval points of the capstone.

Where we are

Ask Rasoi has a brain and a memory: retrieval with citations, filtered by who is asking, dated to today, behind search_policy.

What it still cannot do: look in Bahi. It cannot see that Shakti Seals is not in the supplier master, or that Rasoi has 54,000 gaskets.
