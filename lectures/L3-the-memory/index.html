Section 6 of 11 · RAG from scratch
Last sectionPasting everything does not fit and costs too much. So: find the few passages that matter.

How do you find three paragraphs in 28,953 chunks?

RAG: search first, then answer from what you found

Six steps. The first three happen once; the last three on every question.

Once · 1

Parse

Each file into units with a location: a PDF section, a sheet row, a service.
Once · 2

Chunk

Units into pieces of about 200 tokens, each with its document’s header.
Once · 3

Index

A keyword index (BM25) and vectors in a vector database (Chroma).
Every question · 4

Search

The question against the index: the top 20 candidates.
Every question · 5

Rerank

A model reads the question with each candidate and reorders them; keep 10.
Every question · 6

Answer

The model answers only from those passages, citing each one.
Fig. 1. RAG as we built it: 693 files, 51,325 units, chunks of about 200 tokens. The race winner searches with the BM25 side; the grid measured both.

Read itThe structured parser wins by about seven points. Small chunks win: a 200-token chunk is about one clause, so the match is not diluted by the rest of the page.

Embeddings: meaning as numbers

An embedding model turns a passage into a vector: 384 or 768 numbers. Similar meaning lands close together.

The question is embedded too; the nearest vectors are the candidates.

BM25: rare words that appear, in short chunks

The keyword ranking every search engine grew up on, in our own code. Click through the formula.

idf: a word in few chunks (ERR-PO-0412) counts far more than one in many (order).
Saturation: the tenth “battery” in a chunk adds little (k1 = 1.2).
Length: long chunks are damped (b = 0.75). Built once for all chunks in s.

Andrej Karpathy, “LLM Wiki”, a GitHub gist, April 2026. Read it: gist.github.com/karpathy LLM Wiki

Layer 1

Layer 2

Markdown pages the LLM writes and owns: one per supplier, policy, screen, error. Plus index.md and log.md.
Layer 3

The schema

AGENTS.md: the conventions every agent follows. Written by people.
Operation

Ingest

Operation

Operation

In the gist’s words: “Obsidian is the IDE, the LLM is the programmer, the wiki is the codebase.” Its ancestors: Vannevar Bush’s Memex (1945), and Wikipedia itself.
Fig. 2. The LLM Wiki for Northbridge: Henrik’s files at the bottom, never edited; the pages the model writes; the schema people write.

Twelve rules, in the order we used them

Each one is a step from today, not a slogan.

Audit the corpus: files, shapes, tokens, the mess.
Collect real questions and sort them: genesis, analytical, documentation.
Set metrics and targets with the users, latency included.
Build a golden set from real questions, with gold sources.
Parse by document type; never one reader for all.
Chunk by structure, small, with each document’s header.
Pick an embedding model by measurement (domain models for medicine).
Use BM25 or hybrid search wherever codes matter.
Filter by permission before ranking, and say what was withheld.
Handle versions and effective dates explicitly.
Choose a wiki when synthesis across documents matters and the corpus changes slowly.
Measure again after every change, in CI.

The same method, four domains

What changes is what you must never get wrong.

Pharma

SOPs, batch records, regulatory filings

Versions: only the approved, effective SOP may answer.

Audit trail: every answer logged with the exact document version.

Codes: product and batch numbers need BM25.

Healthcare

Guidelines, formularies, protocols

Permissions: patient data never in the shared index.

Embeddings: a medical model, measured against a general one.

Refuse outside the documents, always.

Banking

Policies, product terms, circulars

Effective dates: the rule in force on the transaction date.

Wiki for products that combine many circulars.

Latency matters at the branch counter.

Legal

Contracts, clauses, precedents

Citations to the clause, checked by script.

Multi-hop is the norm: a wiki of parties and clauses helps.

Confidential matters filtered before ranking.
