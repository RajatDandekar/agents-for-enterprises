Works on Friday, dies on Monday

Something that works as a demo often fails in the pilot. The model is the same on both days.

Friday · the demo
  • One person, the happy path, a question you have tried before
  • An admin account that can do everything
  • It says “Done”, and everyone claps
  • Nobody opens the ERP to check
Monday · the pilot
  • Two hundred people, and questions nobody tested
  • Real permissions, real limits, real approvers
  • A reply gets lost; the user clicks again
  • The ERP says “Draft”, and finance asks who approved it

Here are four ways agents fail in a pilot. None is specific to one kind of software: they appear in finance, sales, support, manufacturing and HR alike.

How agents fail · 01 / 04

It acts with more power than the person asking

The bot logs in with one all-powerful account, so it can do what the person asking never could.

Financeapproves a payment the clerk could not approve
Salesgrants a discount only the director may give
HRshows salary records the requester may not see
In the real worldJuly 2025: an AI coding agent with full access to a company’s live system deleted its production database during a code freeze.Reported by eWeek and The Register; the vendor apologised and separated test from live systems
Ask yourself
Whose permissions does the bot really have?
Should it ever have more power than you?
How agents fail · 02 / 04

It breaks a rule it has just quoted

It can tell you the policy, and a minute later act against it when you push.

Salesstates the 15% discount cap, then offers 25%
Supportexplains the 30-day refund window, then refunds on day 45
Financesays payments need two signatures, then pays on one
In the real world2024: an airline’s chatbot told a customer about a refund rule the airline did not have. A tribunal held the airline responsible for what its chatbot said.Moffatt v. Air Canada, 2024
Ask yourself
Is the rule only in its instructions, or enforced?
Who is responsible for what it says?
How agents fail · 03 / 04

It does the same thing twice

The action worked, but the reply got lost. So it, or you, tries again.

Financea supplier is paid twice
Supportthe customer gets the same email twice
Operationstwo orders, two shipments
In the real worldPayment systems give every payment a unique key, so that a retried request returns the first result instead of charging the card twice.Stripe’s documentation on idempotent requests
Ask yourself
Was it safe to try again?
Would anyone notice the duplicate?
How agents fail · 04 / 04

It says “done” when it isn’t

It reports what the system said, not what actually happened.

Financean invoice “posted” that is still a draft
Supporta ticket “closed” that never saved
HRleave “approved” that still waits for a second sign-off
In everyday lifeYou have seen this one: an app says “sent” and the message never arrives; a form says “submitted” and nobody ever receives it.
Ask yourself
Did anything check the real result?
What does “done” actually mean?

The turn

None of these is a model problem.

A better model fixes none of them. The system around the model does.

01
Who it acts as. It works as you, with your permissions and limits, never as a super-user.
02
The rules it follows. Written down, and enforced in code at the moment it acts.
03
Doing things exactly once. A retry finds the first result instead of doing it again.
04
Proving it is done. It checks the real result before it tells you.

Building that system is the job this course is named after: agentic systems engineering.

The mistakes people make

You do not need to know anything about agents to recognise these.

Demoing only the easy cases

It works on the five questions you tried. Real users ask the sixth.

Trusting the reply

“Done” is a sentence. Nobody checks what really happened.

One all-powerful login

The fastest way to a demo, and the fastest way to an accident.

Never measuring

No test set, so every change is a guess, and nobody knows what it costs.

Each of these has a lecture in this course. By the end you will avoid all four without thinking.

One shared record

DefinitionAn ERP (enterprise resource planning system) is the one shared record of what a company has, owes, buys, makes and sells, with screens for the people who change it.

Purchasing, stores, finance, production, sales and HR all read and write the same record.

Each group of screens for one department is a module.

Fig. 1. An ERP: modules around one shared record. SAP, Oracle, Dynamics and Odoo all have this shape.

Every document has a status

DefinitionA status says where a document is in its life. Only some moves are allowed.

An order is a Draft until it is submitted.

It waits for approval if it is above the buyer’s limit, then is Approved.

Only Released means the supplier has it. Approved is not enough.

When every battery pack has arrived, it is Closed.

What a screen is

A 1998 ERP is used through screens: one screen per kind of work. This is order entry, PUR-PO-01.

The tree on the left is the menu of screens. Your role decides which ones are greyed out.

Fields hold codes. A red star means required; F2 looks a code up.

Buttons do the work: Fetch, Save, Close.

The status bar at the bottom is the only place the ERP talks back.
