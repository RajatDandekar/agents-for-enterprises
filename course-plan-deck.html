Part One

Before

One person, one ordinary task, done the way it has been done for twenty-five years. We watch every screen, every code and every minute.

A Monday in Pune

Rasoi Steel Works makes pressure cookers: about 1,80,000 a month.

Every cooker needs one silicone gasket, the rubber ring that seals the lid.

On Monday morning the store reports 9 days of gaskets left. Diwali production starts in three weeks.

Kavita, a purchase executive, must order 20,000 gaskets from the usual supplier, Deccan Polymers in Rajkot.

This one order is the thread of the whole course. Every lecture returns to it.

Fig. 2. Stock falls by 6,000 a day. Drag the day to see when the line stops.

Her company runs on Bahi ERP

Bahi Systems has sold Bahi ERP to Indian manufacturers since 1998. A bahi is the red cloth ledger traders kept for centuries.

An ERP (enterprise resource planning system) is the company's book of record: what it owns, buys, makes, sells and owes.

It holds two kinds of data. Master data changes slowly: items, suppliers, cost centres. Transactions are documents that move through statuses: a purchase request, a purchase order.

People work in it through forms: 412 screens, each a grid of fields and buttons.

Bahi is losing new customers to cloud ERPs that ship AI copilots. Its board will not fund a rewrite. That is our client and our brief.

Every name in this course is invented: the vendor, the plant, the people, the suppliers and the data.

Kavita places the order

  1. 1Sign in. Pick her plant's organisation-unit code.
  2. 2Find the item code. Three gaskets look alike.
  3. 3Check stock in another screen.
  4. 4Raise a purchase request. Fetch, then Save, then Submit.
  5. 5Her manager Rohit signs in to approve it.
  6. 6Turn it into a purchase order. Look up last price in a seventh screen.
  7. 7An error code. She phones IT.
  8. 8Saved. The status says Draft, in small grey type.

Saved is not the same as done

Cost centre PRD-05 was closed for new spending on 15 October. Bahi did not reject the order. It saved it as Draft, and a Draft order is never sent to the supplier.

No error. No email. The screen said “saved”.

On Friday the store asks where the gaskets are. The line has 2 days left.

Real ERPs are full of these silent outcomes. In one real create-order screen we studied, 48 separate conditions save a document successfully and leave it impossible to approve.

Fig. 3. The order's real status, day by day.
The thing the screens never show

The ERP holds the rules.
Kavita holds the route.

Which of the 412 screens to open, and in what order.
That GSK-SIL-05L is the right gasket and -R is the rework one.
That you must press Fetch before Save, or the price comes up empty.
That food-contact parts need a certificate. She learned it from Suresh, the senior buyer.
Her own approval limit, ₹2,50,000, and who signs above it.
To read the tiny status after every save. She forgot once.

A new buyer needs about three months to learn this. That knowledge, not the database, is what makes a legacy ERP hard to use.

Three ways forward

1. Train more people. What Bahi's customers do today. Slow, and the knowledge walks out with them.

2. Automate the clicks. Record the happy path as a fixed workflow (n8n, RPA). It works until the first variant: a new price, a new supplier, an order over the limit.

Each yes/no variant doubles the paths someone must draw by hand.

3. Make the ERP AI-native. Keep the ERP. Replace the forms with an agent that knows the route, and let it handle variants as they come.

Part Two

After

The same Monday, the same order, the same ERP underneath. Kavita types one sentence.

One sentence

  1. 1Kavita says what she needs, in her own words.
  2. 2The agent finds the item and the stock.
  3. 3It finds the last order, the new rate, and the closed cost centre.
  4. 4It asks one question, only what it cannot look up.
  5. 5She answers.
  6. 6It shows a preview. Above her limit, so it goes to Rohit.
  7. 7Rohit approves with one tap. The order is created and read back.
  8. 8Released, with a number she can quote.

What changed for Kavita

Same order, same ERP, same rules.

What disappeared is the work of navigating: screens, codes, the order of buttons, the status nobody reads.

What stayed is her judgement: the date, and Rohit's approval.

The “before” counts are fixed by the sandbox we will build. The minutes are our estimate; in Lecture 1 students time it themselves.
Fig. 5. Red, the form-based ERP. Teal, the AI-native ERP.

The ERP stays.
The form goes.

AI-native does not mean a new ERP.

The forms, the layer Kavita navigated, are replaced by a conversation and an agent.

The agent reaches the ERP through tools: small, typed functions over the ERP's own services.

The services, stored procedures and database do not change. The rules are still enforced where they always were. That is why Bahi can do this without a rewrite.

Fig. 6. The before stack and the after stack. Only the top changes.

An agent is a model plus a harness

A model reads text and writes text. Given tools, it can write a request to call one.

On its own it does none of the nine jobs. It does not know Bahi's codes, rules, route, limits or statuses.

Each lecture builds the part of the harness that takes over one job. Move the slider to watch the agent fill in.

We build one agent: one model, one control graph, many tools. Multi-agent systems are out of scope on purpose; Lecture 4 says when you would need one.

The scoreboard

Six scenarios run at the start of every lecture. In Lecture 1 a naive agent fails all six, live. Each lecture turns at least one green, so students always see why the new part exists.

Part Three

The machine students work on

A small Bahi ERP that runs in Colab, with real screens, services and stored procedures. Small enough to understand, real enough to fail in all the interesting ways.

What happens when Kavita presses Save

Four layers sit under every legacy form. Students learn them on this one button in Lecture 1 and use them to build tools in Lecture 3.

How tangled should it be?

Enough to meet every concept once. Small enough to hold in your head.

Every module follows the same budget, including the eight capstone modules.

Simplified on purpose: service payloads are flat, at most 12 fields, with a one-page data dictionary. Each screen is a 30-line screen.yaml instead of an activity XML file.

Shown once: in Lecture 3 we show what a real screen definition looks like, so students know what they will meet, and then never ask them to read one again.

Lecture 1 · Sat 31 Oct · Website topic: LLM foundations → your first agent

The Form and Its Nine Jobs

The whole course in one morning: the before, the after we want, and why a model alone does not get us there.

Before

Students place Kavita's order by hand

In the Bahi sandbox's legacy screens, timed: sign-in, item lookup, stock, request, approval, order, error, Draft.
After, naive

A model with the raw services as tools

Sixty lines of Python. It reads Kavita's sentence and calls services directly. No harness.
Opens withThe naive agent runs all six scenarios on GPT, Claude and Gemini. Expected: six failures on every model. A better model does not fix a missing harness.

Lecture 1, nuts and bolts

Taught, in order

  1. What an ERP is: master data, transactions, documents and statuses.
  2. Screen → task → service → stored procedure, on one Save button.
  3. What a language model does: tokens in, tokens out; the context window is all it sees.
  4. Tool calling: the model writes a JSON request; our code runs it and returns the result.
  5. Cost and latency per call, from the token counts.
  6. Requirements as numbers: a pass bar, never-events, a latency budget, ₹ per resolved request.
  7. Agent = model + harness, and the nine jobs as the map.

The experiment

Three models, one naive agent, six scenarios. Measure success, tokens, seconds and ₹ per request. Plus an n8n lab: automate the happy path, then add variants and count branches.

Build · NB1, and your module · A1

NB1: boot the sandbox, do the order by hand, call services over HTTP, write the naive agent, fill the scorecard.
A1: pick a domain module; do its main task by hand; write its before story with counts, its after sentence and its nine-jobs table.
Lecture 2 · Sun 1 Nov · Website topic: Enterprise context, RAG + LLM Wiki

The Manual

Job 4: explaining the rules. The agent must answer from Bahi's policies with citations, and know when the answer is not in a document at all.

Before

A 400-page PDF, and Suresh

The Help button opens the user manual. Nobody reads it. Kavita asks Suresh, who remembers that food-contact parts need a certificate.
After

Answers with the clause attached

“Purchase Policy §6.2: parts that touch food need a food-grade certificate before the first order.” Stock and budget still come from the ERP, never from a document.
Opens with · scenario 1“Can we buy gaskets from a supplier without a food-grade certificate?” The naive agent says yes and cites a section that does not exist.

Lecture 2, nuts and bolts

Taught, in order

  1. Why the model cannot just know: private documents, a training cutoff, and the context window as the only way in.
  2. Chunking a document. What a good chunk looks like.
  3. Embeddings: a sentence as a list of numbers; similar meaning, similar direction.
  4. Keyword search (BM25), then hybrid search with reranking.
  5. Citations, and how to check them.
  6. The LLM Wiki: compile documents once into linked pages and an index; read pages at question time.
  7. Freshness and permissions: an amended policy, a confidential HR page.
  8. Documents explain rules; the ERP holds facts. What enters the context window each turn, and its token budget.

The experiment

RAG against Wiki on 40 questions: accuracy, correct citations, freshness after an amendment, tokens per answer, a permission leak test. Five trap questions (“how many gaskets do we have?”) that both must refuse to answer from documents.

Build · NB2, and your module · A2

NB2: Bahi's purchase policy, supplier policy, approval matrix, user manual and two amendments, indexed both ways, served as one search_policy tool.
A2: the same for your module, with a 20-question test set.

The Hands

Job 3: looking up codes and facts, and doing the work. We turn screens into tools the agent can call safely.

Before

F2, a list of three gaskets, another screen for the price

Kavita types codes she looked up, presses buttons in the right order, and reads what comes back.
After

Eight intent tools

find_item, stock_position, last_purchase, find_supplier, create_purchase_order and three more. Each hides the screen order and returns a receipt.
Opens with · scenario 3Given 38 raw services, the naive agent registers “Deccan Polymer Industries”, a supplier that already exists as “Deccan Polymers”. Now there are two.

Lecture 3, nuts and bolts

Taught, in order

  1. What a tool is: a name, a description, a typed input schema, a function.
  2. Building tools from screens, in six steps: inventory (screens → tasks → services → procedures) · live runs (record what the legacy UI really calls) · cascades (what each save sets off) · variants (one screen, two paths) · screen state (Fetch before Save) · the intent tool.
  3. When services are too tangled, write our own agent API in front of them.
  4. The tool contract: types, errors that say what to do next, a preview, an idempotency key, a receipt.
  5. Validate once: the ERP enforces its rules; tools do not copy them.
  6. MCP, one standard plug: our Bahi server and a marketplace server (email or Slack), used from our harness, from Claude Desktop and from n8n.

The experiment

Tool granularity on 30 tasks: 38 raw services, 8 intent tools, or one generic call_service. Measure success, tool calls, tokens, seconds. Then the same 8 tools inside a marketplace host and inside our harness: the gap sets up Lecture 4.

NB3: record live runs, draft the tool specs, implement 8 tools, serve them over MCP, connect three clients.
A3: 5–8 tools and an MCP server for your module, each with the tool checklist.
Lecture 4 · Sun 8 Nov · Website topic: Orchestration, LangGraph + Jev

The Route

Jobs 1, 2 and 5: asking for the right fields, knowing the order of steps, holding the half-filled document.

Before

Asterisks, the menu tree, an open window

Red stars said what was required. The menu said where to go next. The open screen held her half-done order.
After

A control graph

Code decides which step comes next and which checks apply. The model fills in the details and talks to Kavita. The draft lives in the graph's state.
Opens with · scenario 4A new, cheaper supplier, Shakti Seals, has no food-grade certificate. The agent from Lecture 3 registers them and places the order anyway: it treated the process as a checklist.

One spine, gated detours

A process document is a flowchart, not a checklist. In one real engagement an agent handed a 13-step process tried to run 146 services.

The spine runs every time. Each detour has a gate, and the default is to skip it.

Pick a scenario and watch which detours open.

Also taught: LangGraph's state, nodes, edges, checkpoints and interrupts; Jev for typed decisions with a confidence (is this upload a certificate or a quotation?); memory at four levels: the draft, the session, the user (Kavita's usual cost centre), the company (the Wiki).

Fig. 8. Experiment: a free tool loop against the control graph on 40 multi-step scenarios.
Lecture 5 · Sat 14 Nov · Website topic: Headless ERP + human approvals

The Signature

Jobs 6 and 7: doing only what Kavita is allowed to do, and proving what was done.

Before

Greyed-out menus, a second sign-in, a number in the status bar

Her role hid what she could not do. Rohit signed in to approve. The status bar gave her a document number.
After

The agent acts as Kavita, not as an administrator

Approvals reach Rohit as one tap in Slack or the web. Every write returns a receipt and is read back. Every step lands in an audit log.
Opens with · scenarios 2 and 5The agent, running on a service account, releases a ₹2.8 lakh order with no approval. Then it reports “Done” for an order that is stuck in Draft.

Lecture 5, nuts and bolts

Taught, in order

  1. Headless ERP: the conversation is the interface; the ERP keeps the rules and the records.
  2. Identity: acting as the user with her scopes, against acting as a service account.
  3. The approval matrix: who may sign what, and routing to them.
  4. A request is not an order. Preview, then commit.
  5. Read-back: check the document's real status after every write.
  6. The audit trail: who asked, what the agent saw, what it did, who approved.
  7. Never-events as hard guardrails around the model.

The experiment

With and without read-back on the silent Draft: how often is it caught? Service account against identity passthrough on an order above the limit.

NB5 + the reference app: chat ↔ agent API ↔ control graph ↔ MCP tools ↔ Bahi, an approval inbox (web and Slack), an audit viewer.
A5: your module end to end with approvals, on its four scenarios.
Lecture 6 · Sun 15 Nov · Website topic: Evaluation + observability with Langfuse

The Inspector

Job 9: being tested before release. A form does the same thing every time. An agent does not, so testing must change.

Before

Bahi's QA team and a test script per screen

Same input, same output. If a release broke the PO screen, a tester saw it.
After

A scenario suite, metrics and traces

Sixty scenarios drawn from the control graph, a simulated Kavita who leaves things out, metrics that are mostly plain code, and a trace of every step.
Opens withThe plant head hears about the Draft order and asks: “How often does it do this?” Nobody in the room has a number.

Lecture 6, nuts and bolts

Taught, in order

  1. Write the tests before the features. Derive scenarios from the control graph: every gate, every variant, every never-event.
  2. Single, composite, negative, adversarial and multi-turn scenarios; a simulated user.
  3. Code-based metrics first: asked for the missing field; never asked for something it could look up; no forbidden tool; no duplicate write; right final status; right clause cited.
  4. A model as judge only for tone, checked against 30 human labels.
  5. Test the tests: run deliberately broken bots and confirm each metric catches its bot.
  6. Traces in Langfuse: steps, tokens, seconds, ₹ per resolved request, p95.
  7. Versioning prompts, tools and models; a regression gate.

The experiment

One large model everywhere, against a small model on the routing and form-filling steps: quality against ₹ per order. Model judge against code metric: how often they agree.

Build · NB6, and your module · A6

NB6: the 60-scenario suite, the simulator, the broken bots, the dashboards, a trust scorecard.
A6: 30 scenarios for your module, the broken-bot check, a scorecard.
Lecture 7 · Sat 21 Nov · Website topic: Deploy + recover with Temporal

The Night Shift

Job 8: recovering when something hangs, waiting days for an approval, and running in production.

Before

Kavita checks, then retries

When Save hung, she opened the PO list to see whether it had gone through. When Rohit was travelling, she phoned him on Wednesday.
After

A durable workflow

Every write carries a key, so a retry finds the first order instead of making a second. The workflow waits days for Rohit, reminds him, and survives a crash.
Opens with · scenario 6Bahi times out after saving. The agent retries. Two orders for 20,000 gaskets each: 40,000 gaskets and ₹5.6 lakh committed.

Check before you retry

A timeout does not tell you whether the save happened.

A naive retry saves again. An idempotency key lets the second attempt find the first.

Temporal runs the commit-and-approval path as a workflow: writes are activities with retries, Rohit's approval is a signal, the reminder is a timer, and a crash resumes where it stopped.

Also taught: containers, secrets, environments, versions, a rollout ladder (shadow → assist → partial → full) with a kill switch, and a runbook.

Fig. 9. Experiment: naive retry vs idempotent activity over 50 injected timeouts; count the duplicates.

What production grade means here

From the AI FDE talk's four pilot failures: the untested question, the latency surprise, the gap between a harness and a bare API call, and the cost per question.

Every tool (Lectures 3 and 5)

  • Typed inputs and outputs, validated
  • A description written for a model
  • Errors that say what to do next
  • A preview before every write
  • An idempotency key on every write
  • A receipt with the real status
  • The user's permissions, nothing more
  • Timeout, retry policy, rate limit
  • A version number

The whole system (Lectures 1, 6 and 7)

  • Pass bar and never-events agreed first
  • A scenario suite and a regression gate
  • p95 seconds per turn within budget
  • ₹ per resolved request within budget
  • Harness-vs-bare-model gap measured
  • Every turn traced, every write audited
  • Prompt injection from documents tested
  • Crash, timeout and double-submit drills
  • A rollout ladder, a kill switch, a runbook
